# E-Commerce Demand Forecasting & Inventory Decision System

An end-to-end data science project: forecast daily product-level demand
for an e-commerce retailer, validate the forecasts the way a time-series
problem requires, and turn the forecasts into an inventory (reorder point
/ safety stock) decision with scenario analysis.

Built as an interview-grade portfolio project targeting a **Data
Scientist Intern** role (forecasting/prediction, statistical modeling,
SQL, model selection/training/cross-validation, deployment-at-scale
thinking).

## Business Problem

A retailer needs to estimate future product demand so inventory can be
planned more effectively — order the right amount, avoid stockouts,
avoid overstock. This project answers:

> **Given historical sales data, how can we forecast future demand and
> use those forecasts to make better inventory decisions?**

## Objective

Predict **daily demand for each product** (`(date, product_id) →
quantity`), and use the forecast to drive a simple, explicit inventory
policy (reorder point + safety stock) that is stress-tested under
demand scenarios.

## Dataset

`data/sales_raw.csv` — **synthetic** daily sales data, generated by
`src/generate_data.py`, built to have the structure a real retail
extract has:

- 40 products across 4 categories (Electronics, Home & Kitchen, Apparel, Beauty)
- Daily granularity, **Jan 2023 – Jun 2025** (~2.5 years, 36k+ rows)
- Per-product trend, weekly seasonality, yearly seasonality, holiday
  spikes, promotion/discount events, and per-product noise
- Intentionally injected data-quality problems (missing values,
  duplicate rows, invalid negative quantities/prices, bad date values)
  so the data-quality step has real issues to find — see `data/README.md`

Real historical retail data with this much history and no
proprietary/PII exposure is hard to obtain publicly at this
granularity, so synthetic data with realistic structure and realistic
messiness was used instead — this is explicit and documented, not
hidden (see **Limitations**).

## Data Quality

`notebooks/01_data_quality.ipynb` loads the raw export and checks:
missing values, duplicate `(date, product_id)` rows, invalid
negative quantities/prices, and bad/out-of-range dates. Problems found
are removed or corrected (not silently dropped) and the result is
saved to `data/sales_clean.csv`, which everything downstream uses.

## SQL Analysis

`sql/sales_analysis.sql` — eight business questions answered in SQL
against the cleaned table (written for SQLite via
`src/run_sql_analysis.py` for a zero-setup demo; portable to
Postgres/Redshift/MySQL, see notes in the file):

1. Total sales (units + revenue) by month
2. Top products by units sold
3. Top categories by revenue
4. Most volatile products (coefficient of variation)
5. Average daily demand by product
6. Month-over-month growth rate
7. Demand by day of week
8. Products with declining demand (recent 90d vs prior 90d)

Full executed output for every query: `sql/query_results.txt`.

## Exploratory Analysis

`notebooks/02_time_series_eda.ipynb` — daily/weekly/monthly demand,
demand by product and category, weekday and monthly seasonality, and an
outlier/spike example. Figures saved to `reports/figures/`.

## Feature Engineering

`notebooks/03_feature_engineering.ipynb` / `src/features.py` — lag
features (`lag_1, lag_7, lag_14, lag_28`), rolling statistics
(`rolling_mean/std_7/14/28`), and calendar features
(`day_of_week, month, week_of_year, is_weekend`), all built so that a
feature for date *t* only uses information available strictly before
*t* (no future leakage — see below). Output: `data/model_features.csv`.

### Leakage, explicitly

A feature for predicting January 20 demand can only use information
that existed on or before January 19 (or January 20 itself if it's
known in advance, like a scheduled promotion or the calendar date).
Lags and rolling windows are shifted before being computed so that
`lag_1` on Jan 20 is Jan 19's demand, never Jan 20's or later.

## Forecasting Models

`notebooks/04_forecasting_models.ipynb`, `src/forecasting.py`:

1. **Seasonal Naive baseline** — predict today's demand as demand from
   the same weekday one week ago. Every model below must beat this to
   be worth using.
2. **Ridge Regression** — linear baseline on the engineered features.
3. **Random Forest**
4. **Gradient Boosting** (`sklearn.ensemble.HistGradientBoostingRegressor`
   — a histogram-based gradient boosting implementation, algorithmically
   the same family as XGBoost/LightGBM; XGBoost itself isn't installable
   in this offline sandbox, and this is disclosed rather than silently
   substituted — see Limitations. Swapping in real XGBoost is a one-line
   change once the package is available.)

## Validation Strategy

**No `train_test_split(random_state=42)`.** Time series data must be
split so training only ever sees the past relative to what it predicts:

- A single **60-day chronological holdout** at the end of the series
  for model comparison (`notebooks/04`)
- A **4-fold expanding-window time-series cross-validation**
  (`notebooks/05`, `src/forecasting.py::time_series_folds`) so the
  holdout comparison isn't just one lucky/unlucky window

## Evaluation Metrics

- **MAE** — mean absolute error, in units, easy to explain to a
  non-technical stakeholder ("on average, forecasts were off by N units")
- **RMSE** — penalizes large misses more heavily
- **sMAPE** — a bounded percentage-error metric, more stable than MAPE
  when demand can be near zero

## Results

Holdout (last 60 days), from `reports/model_comparison_holdout.csv`:

| Model | MAE | RMSE | sMAPE % |
|---|--:|--:|--:|
| Gradient Boosting | 15.79 | 23.02 | 20.05 |
| Random Forest | 15.82 | 23.00 | 20.06 |
| Ridge Regression | 16.64 | 23.82 | 21.14 |
| Seasonal Naive | 23.13 | 34.25 | 28.58 |

4-fold expanding-window CV (mean ± std), from
`reports/cv_results_summary.csv`, confirms the same ranking holds
across folds, not just the one holdout window: Gradient Boosting and
Random Forest both land around **MAE ≈ 15.4 ± 1.4**, Ridge around
**16.0 ± 1.1**, Seasonal Naive around **22.9 ± 1.4** — i.e. the
tree-based models cut error by roughly a third versus the naive
baseline, consistently, not by luck on one split.

All numbers above come directly from the CSVs in `reports/` — generated
by actually running the notebooks/scripts in this repo, not invented.

## Error Analysis

`notebooks/05_model_evaluation.ipynb`, `reports/error_by_product.csv` —
per-product error breakdown. The model's error is not uniform: products
with the highest average demand and the most volatile demand patterns
(promotion-heavy, spiky SKUs) have the largest MAE, while stable,
lower-volume products are forecast much more accurately. That's a
genuine, actionable finding — forecast-improvement effort (better
features, product-specific models) is best spent on the volatile,
high-volume subset, not spread evenly across all 40 products.

## Inventory Simulation

`notebooks/06_inventory_simulation.ipynb` — turns the Gradient Boosting
forecast into a periodic-review reorder-point / order-up-to policy per
product (safety stock sized from each product's own forecast-error
standard deviation, not raw demand variance), then simulates day-by-day
inventory under five scenarios:

| Scenario | Avg service level | Total lost units |
|---|--:|--:|
| A — Normal demand | 80.3% | 34,897 |
| B — Demand +10% | 76.8% | 46,287 |
| C — Demand +20% | 72.2% | 61,149 |
| D — Reduced capacity (70%) | 76.2% | 46,583 |
| E — Promotion spike | 76.1% | 59,299 |

(from `reports/inventory_scenario_summary.csv`, generated by the
notebook, not invented)

**A finding worth stating plainly:** the policy targets a ~95% cycle
service level but Scenario A lands at ~80%. The notebook diagnoses why:
safety stock was sized using `sqrt(lead_time)` demand-uncertainty, but
under a periodic-review policy the true exposure window before the next
order can arrive is `lead_time + review_period` — so the buffer needs
`sqrt(lead_time + review_period)` instead. Naming and explaining a gap
like this is exactly the kind of thing worth being able to defend in an
interview, rather than a simulation that quietly "just works."

## Business Recommendations

1. Adopt the Gradient Boosting forecast for demand planning — it beats
   the naive baseline by roughly a third on MAE, consistently across CV
   folds.
2. Size safety stock per-product from forecast-error std, and correct
   the safety-stock time horizon to cover the full
   `lead_time + review_period` exposure window (see Inventory
   Simulation above).
3. Give known promotions their own uplift/buffer rather than leaning on
   routine safety stock — the promotion-spike scenario shows routine
   buffers are not sized for promotional demand.
4. Re-forecast on the same cadence as the review period so a sustained
   demand shift is absorbed by an updated forecast, not a permanently
   inflated buffer.
5. Focus forecast-improvement effort on the high-volume, high-volatility
   products flagged in the per-product error analysis.

## Limitations

- **Synthetic dataset.** Built for realistic structure and realistic
  messiness, but real retail data has correlations (cannibalization
  between products, cross-product substitution, supplier constraints)
  that a per-product synthetic generator does not capture.
- **XGBoost not installed** in this offline environment; used
  `HistGradientBoostingRegressor` (same algorithm family) instead. Swap
  in real `xgboost.XGBRegressor` where the package is available —
  `src/forecasting.py` is written so this is a one-line change.
- **Inventory simulation is a simplified policy simulation**, not a
  production system: single lead time and review period for every
  product, no supplier minimum order quantities or holding/stockout
  cost trade-off, no cross-product capacity sharing.
- **Promotion information is available to the model at forecast time**
  in this dataset, which is more favorable than many real deployments
  where promotion calendars arrive late or incomplete.

## Future Work

- Swap in real XGBoost/LightGBM and compare against
  `HistGradientBoostingRegressor`.
- Per-product or per-category models instead of one pooled model.
- Prediction intervals (quantile regression / conformal prediction)
  instead of a single point forecast, feeding directly into the safety
  stock calculation instead of a separate error-std step.
- Cost-based inventory optimization (holding cost vs stockout cost)
  instead of a fixed service-level target.
- Deployment sketch: batch daily retraining + scoring job, forecasts
  written to a table an inventory system reads from.

## Interview Talking Points (self-check)

- **Why is this a time-series problem?** Observations are ordered in
  time and autocorrelated (today's demand depends on recent demand and
  calendar position), and the goal is predicting the future from the
  past — a random train/test split would leak future information into
  training and overstate accuracy.
- **Why a seasonal-naive baseline first?** A complex model's error
  number is meaningless in isolation; it has to beat a trivial
  reference or it isn't earning its complexity.
- **Why `lag_7`?** Weekly seasonality (weekday shopping patterns
  repeat) is one of the strongest signals in retail demand; last
  week's same-weekday value is a strong, always-available predictor.
- **Why can't future demand be a feature?** It would not exist yet at
  prediction time in production — using it in training creates
  leakage that inflates offline accuracy and collapses in production.
- **Why can't you randomly split the data?** It breaks the
  past-predicts-future structure and lets the model train on
  observations from *after* the point it's being tested on.
- **Why Gradient Boosting/Random Forest over linear regression?** They
  capture non-linear interactions (e.g., promotion effect differs by
  product/season) without manually specifying every interaction term;
  the results table shows the tree-based models beating Ridge here.
- **Why MAE as the headline metric?** It's in the same units as
  demand and is directly interpretable by a non-technical
  stakeholder; RMSE and sMAPE are reported alongside for a fuller
  picture.
- **How would a retailer use this forecast?** As the demand-planning
  input to an inventory policy — feeding the reorder-point/safety-stock
  calculation in notebook 06, not as an end in itself.
- **Where does the model fail?** High-volume, high-volatility,
  promotion-heavy products, per the error-by-product analysis.
- **How did you convert a forecast into an inventory decision?**
  Reorder point = forecasted demand over lead time + safety stock
  (from forecast-error std); target level extends that to the full
  review-period exposure window; see notebook 06 for the worked
  policy and the periodic-review safety-stock correction it surfaces.
- **What assumptions did you make?** See Limitations above — synthetic
  data, algorithm substitution for XGBoost, simplified single lead
  time/review period, no cost trade-off, promotions visible to the
  forecast.
