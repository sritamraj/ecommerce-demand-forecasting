\# E-Commerce Demand Forecasting \& Inventory Decision System



An end-to-end data science project that forecasts daily product-level demand, validates the forecasts using chronological time-series methods, and converts the forecast into an inventory decision policy with stress testing.



The project demonstrates forecasting, statistical modeling, SQL analysis, feature engineering, model selection, time-series cross-validation, evaluation integrity, and inventory decision analysis.



\---



\## Business Problem



A retailer needs to estimate future product demand so inventory can be planned more effectively — balancing the risk of stockouts against excess inventory.



This project asks:



> \*\*Given historical sales data, how can we forecast future demand and use those forecasts as an input to an explicit inventory policy?\*\*



\---



\## Objective



Predict daily demand for each product:



`(date, product\_id) → quantity`



and use the resulting forecast to drive a periodic-review inventory policy based on:



\* reorder point

\* safety stock

\* lead time

\* review period

\* forecast-error uncertainty



The inventory policy is then evaluated under normal demand and demand/capacity stress scenarios.

\## Leakage Prevention



The forecasting problem is treated as a \*\*rolling one-step-ahead forecasting problem\*\*.



For a prediction on date `t`, historical demand features only use information available before `t`.



For example:



```text

lag\_1 on January 20 = demand on January 19

```



and rolling features are calculated on shifted demand so that the target day's demand is never included in its own predictors.



Calendar information and scheduled promotion information are treated as known at prediction time.



The final evaluation period is never used to construct forecast-error uncertainty for the inventory policy.

\# Forecasting Models



Implemented in:



```text

notebooks/04\_forecasting\_models.ipynb

src/forecasting.py

```



The models are:



\### Seasonal Naive



Uses demand from the same product seven days earlier as the prediction.



This provides a simple weekly-seasonality baseline.



\### Ridge Regression



A linear model using the engineered lag, rolling, calendar, price, promotion, discount, and holiday features.



\### Random Forest



A nonlinear tree-based ensemble.



\### HistGradientBoostingRegressor



A histogram-based gradient boosting model from scikit-learn.



It belongs to the same broad gradient-boosted-tree family as XGBoost and LightGBM, but it is \*\*not XGBoost itself\*\*.



XGBoost was unavailable in the offline environment, so `HistGradientBoostingRegressor` was used as the reproducible gradient-boosting implementation.

\# Validation Strategy



Random `train\_test\_split` is deliberately not used.



The project uses chronological evaluation because the forecasting task is:



```text

past → future

```



rather than independently distributed observations.



\## Development / Final Test Split



The final \*\*60 days\*\* are kept as an untouched chronological test period:



```text

Final test:

2025-05-02 → 2025-06-30

```



The development period ends on:



```text

2025-05-01

```



The final test set is not used for model selection or inventory uncertainty calibration.



\## Expanding-Window Cross-Validation



Four chronological validation folds are run over the development period:



```text

Fold 1: 2024-09-04 → 2024-11-02

Fold 2: 2024-11-03 → 2025-01-01

Fold 3: 2025-01-02 → 2025-03-02

Fold 4: 2025-03-03 → 2025-05-01

```



Each fold trains on earlier observations and validates on the subsequent time window.



Implementation:



```text

src/forecasting.py::time\_series\_folds

src/run\_time\_series\_cv.py

```



\---



\# Evaluation Metrics



\### MAE



Mean absolute error, measured in demand units.



This is directly interpretable:



> On average, how many units was the forecast away from actual demand?



\### RMSE



Root mean squared error, which gives greater weight to larger forecast errors.



\### sMAPE



A bounded percentage-error metric used here as an additional relative-error measure.



The project uses a small epsilon in the implementation to improve numerical stability when demand is near zero.

\# Error Analysis



Per-product error analysis is used to inspect whether forecasting error is distributed uniformly across products.



The project examines product-level absolute error and demand characteristics to identify products where forecast performance may require additional attention.



The important operational point is that aggregate model metrics can hide product-level variation. A retailer would therefore want to monitor forecast error by SKU rather than relying only on one portfolio-level MAE.

\# Inventory Decision System



`notebooks/06\_inventory\_simulation.ipynb` converts the frozen Gradient Boosting forecast into a simplified periodic-review inventory policy.



The implementation is also available in:



```text

src/inventory\_simulation.py

```



The policy uses:



\* 3-day lead time

\* 7-day review period

\* 10-day protection period

\* service-level z-value of 1.65

\* product-specific forecast-error uncertainty



\## Forecast Uncertainty



Safety-stock uncertainty is estimated from \*\*development-period out-of-fold Gradient Boosting predictions\*\*:



```text

reports/gradient\_boosting\_oof\_predictions.csv

```



This is important for evaluation integrity.



The final 60-day test residuals are \*\*not\*\* used to calibrate safety stock.



\---



\## Protection Period



Because inventory is reviewed every seven days and replenishment takes three days, the policy uses:



```text

protection period = lead time + review period

&#x20;                = 3 + 7

&#x20;                = 10 days

```



The safety-stock calculation therefore accounts for uncertainty across the full protection period.



The implementation assumes approximately independent daily forecast errors when scaling daily error standard deviation across the protection period. This is a simplifying assumption rather than a claim about real retail demand.



\---



\# Inventory Simulation Methodology



The simulation is a \*\*frozen-policy evaluation\*\* over the final 60-day test period.



The forecast plan is fixed for the simulation rather than dynamically reforecasting every review cycle.



Scenario multipliers modify actual demand to stress-test the policy.



The simulation reports:



\* average inventory

\* stockout days

\* cycle service level

\* lost units

\* units ordered

\* reorder point

\* target inventory level

\* safety stock



\### Service metric



The primary service metric is \*\*cycle service level\*\*:



> percentage of simulated days without a stockout.



It is not the same as fill rate.

\# Business Interpretation



The project demonstrates how a demand forecast can become an operational input rather than an isolated model metric.



The analysis shows:



\* Forecast quality varies across products.

\* Tree-based models substantially improve error relative to the seasonal-naive baseline on this synthetic dataset.

\* Inventory service degrades as demand is shifted upward relative to the frozen forecast.

\* Unexpected short-duration demand spikes can create substantial lost demand.

\* Constraining inventory capacity can materially reduce service levels.

\* Forecast uncertainty can be incorporated into safety-stock calculations using out-of-fold residuals.



The results should be interpreted as a methodological demonstration on synthetic data, not as evidence of expected performance on a real retailer's data.

\# Limitations



\### Synthetic dataset



The data generator creates realistic patterns and injected data-quality problems, but it does not reproduce all real retail relationships.



Examples not modeled include:



\* product cannibalization

\* cross-product substitution

\* supplier constraints

\* stock-dependent observed demand

\* supplier lead-time variability

\* real promotion planning processes



\### Gradient boosting implementation



XGBoost was unavailable in the offline environment.



The project therefore uses:



```text

sklearn.ensemble.HistGradientBoostingRegressor

```



This is a gradient-boosted-tree implementation, but it should not be described as XGBoost itself.



\### Simplified inventory policy



The inventory simulation uses common lead time and review period assumptions for all products and does not model:



\* supplier minimum order quantities

\* purchase-order costs

\* holding costs

\* explicit stockout penalties

\* multiple supplier constraints

\* shared warehouse capacity across products

\* stochastic lead times



\### Frozen-policy simulation



The inventory scenarios evaluate a fixed forecast plan over the 60-day test period.



A production implementation would normally refresh forecasts as new observations arrive.



\### Promotion information



Scheduled promotion information is assumed to be known when forecasting.



Real-world promotion calendars can be incomplete, delayed, or changed after the forecast is produced.



\### Forecast uncertainty



The safety-stock calculation uses forecast-error standard deviation and assumes approximately independent daily forecast errors when scaling uncertainty across the protection period.



A production system could replace this with calibrated prediction intervals, quantile forecasts, or conformal methods.

\# Interview Talking Points



\### Why is this a time-series problem?



Observations are ordered in time, demand has temporal dependence and seasonality, and the production task is to predict future observations using information available before those observations occur.



\### Why use a seasonal-naive baseline?



A model should be compared against a simple operationally plausible baseline. Otherwise, a model's absolute error does not tell us whether the additional complexity is useful.



\### Why use `lag\_7`?



Retail demand often has weekly patterns. The previous week's same weekday is therefore a natural baseline and predictor.



\### Why can't future demand be used as a feature?



Because it would not exist at prediction time. Including it creates leakage and produces an offline metric that cannot be reproduced in production.



\### Why not randomly split the data?



Random splitting breaks the chronological structure and can allow training observations to come from periods later than the validation observations.



\### Why use tree-based models?



They can capture nonlinear relationships and interactions between lagged demand, calendar variables, promotions, discounts, and other predictors without manually specifying every interaction.



\### Why use MAE as the headline metric?



MAE is measured directly in demand units and is easy to communicate to business stakeholders.



\### How was the final model evaluated?



The development period was used for four expanding-window CV folds. The model configuration was then frozen, retrained on the complete development period, and evaluated once on the untouched final 60-day period.



\### How was inventory uncertainty calibrated?



Gradient Boosting out-of-fold predictions from the development period were used to estimate forecast-error variability. The final test residuals were not used for safety-stock calibration.



\### Why is the protection period 10 days?



The policy reviews inventory every seven days and has a three-day replenishment lead time, so the exposure period is modeled as:



```text

3-day lead time + 7-day review period = 10 days

```



\### What does the inventory service metric mean?



Cycle service level measures the percentage of simulated days without a stockout. It is different from fill rate, which measures the fraction of demand fulfilled.



\### What are the main inventory stress tests?



The policy is evaluated under normal demand, +10% demand, +20% demand, an unexpected temporary 3× demand spike, and an inventory-capacity constraint at 70% of the unconstrained target.



\### What assumptions should you defend in an interview?



The main assumptions are synthetic data generation, known-at-forecast-time promotions, fixed lead/review periods, frozen-policy inventory simulation, simplified forecast-error scaling, and the absence of explicit holding/stockout cost optimization.
